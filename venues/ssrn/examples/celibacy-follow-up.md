## Abstract (1,680 characters, 240 words; identical to the PDF)

This paper is a follow-up to "The Nobility and Exemplarity of the Celibate Individual" (Zenodo, DOI 10.5281/zenodo.22058254; PhilArchive NEMTNA). It re-examines the two places where that paper relies on empirical science: the Relational Objection and its reply, which rest on psychological and psychiatric claims, and the critique of the inference from species continuation to an individual duty to reproduce, which touches evolutionary biology. Meta-analytic evidence consistently associates loneliness and social isolation with depression and mortality. Evidence on marital or partnership status and well-being shows associations that are small, heterogeneous across individuals, and contested in their causal reading. Chosen solitude is studied as distinct from loneliness. The original reply largely survives, but it needs two corrections. It must separate loneliness and isolation from chosen solitude explicitly. It must also concede that attachment theory can read "self-sufficiency" as deactivation of the attachment system rather than as autonomy. No study of chosen ascetic celibacy as such was found. In biology, non-reproducing helpers in birds and social insects are explained by kin selection, which supports the original point that not every individual must reproduce. Inclusive fitness theory is itself disputed, however, and current evolutionary theory does not explain sexual desire as an adaptation for the good of the species; the original paper's Schopenhauerian "service of the species" framing should be revised. Disclaimer: the author is not a clinician. This paper interprets published research only; it makes no clinical claims and is not medical advice.
